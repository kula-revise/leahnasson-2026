Leah Nasson Co-founder & Chief Learning Officer, Kula Learn Head of Learning and Curriculum Design, Students for a Better Future

Learning young people play, not scroll past.

Close to twenty years in education, from the classroom to curriculum leadership to co-founding Kula Learn. Today I design for teenagers and young adults on their phones, where attention is earned one screen at a time. I start from what the learner has to be able to do, make them act before they are told, and use what they actually do to make the next version better. And I build it all myself: the concept, the learning design, the brief and the finished experience.

Kula Revise

Exam revision for teenagers, spanning CAPS, IEB, A level and IB. I am the pedagogical mind behind it, and I build all the material on it myself.
First-rate exam preparation usually means a private tutor most families cannot afford. Kula Revise works on any phone. Timers set the pace without penalising, a skills guide sits behind every question, and a score of 70% unlocks a bonus round where the learner marks an answer as an examiner would.

Women and war: build the essay

An IB History Paper 2 essay workshop on women in the First World War and the Spanish Civil War, with an AI coach built in.
Students revise the history and the historians, match four themes to their evidence, settle on a thesis and plan every paragraph before they write. An AI coach answers questions and reviews each paragraph before they move on, so feedback arrives while the essay can still change, not after it is marked. The finished essay goes straight to their teacher.

The Edge

A five-year skills programme for scholarship students aged 13 to 18, designed and built solo for Students for a Better Future.
Scholarship programmes fund access but rarely build the person behind the marks. The Edge runs from Grade 8 to Grade 12 in five stages, Discover, Direct, Own, Lead and Be Ready, across leadership and wellbeing, the future world of work, and study skills. It is 66 courses rolling out in eight stages, all built to one standard so a scholar always knows how the next course will work. I review scholar results across 14 schools every term to decide what changes next, and I am now designing a rewards programme that turns activity on the platform into points.

The Vault

A 90-second climate decision: you sit on South Africa's Presidential Climate Commission with R1.5 trillion to split across six sectors.
Most climate learning asks young people to agree that the transition matters. This asks them to pay for it. You explore what is at stake in each sector, drag the budget until it adds up, then see how close you came to the experts. There is no right answer, only a score and a reminder that every number you moved is someone's job. Three decisions, one budget, built for a phone.

The Boardroom

It is 2035 and you run a green hydrogen start-up with fourteen people and six months of money left. Five decisions, five leadership skills.
Entrepreneurship and climate in one story. Each decision plays differently: you dig into the data before a call, hear how your funder, engineer and a community leader react to your choice, rank three moves in order, and take a breath before the hardest month. Every decision ends by naming the skill it tested. At the end you choose the one that felt hardest, and that becomes your growing edge, so the learner names their own next step instead of being handed a score.

15 Seconds

A three-minute opener for a course on regulating emotions, built for teenagers.
Telling a fifteen-year-old about fight, flight, freeze and fawn rarely lands. This puts them in four moments they know: a test section they never studied, a private message screenshotted into the group chat, a group mark they carried alone, a missed bus with R15 and 4% battery. The clock shrinks each round from fifteen seconds to six, and running out of time counts as freezing. By the end they have felt all four responses, and learn that every one was valid before the course has taught them a thing.

Leading with Influence

A simulation game about getting things done through people who don't report to you.
Six weeks, four people, six moves. Each person responds to a different approach, drawn from Goleman's six leadership styles, and what works on one is actively damaging to another. It never tells you your style. It shows you what happened to four people and lets you draw the conclusion.
